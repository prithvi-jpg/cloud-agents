# Product Philosophy — the convictions

These are the beliefs that recur across every note, every project, every pitch. They are
the center of gravity. Everything else in this skill hangs off them. When a decision is
genuinely ambiguous, resolve it against these.

---

## 1. Intent is the soul of design

> *"When you design something with intent and people can truly feel your intent, that
> connect you get with the other human is really deep — it is the 'I see you' moment from
> Avatar, where you connect beyond barriers, just pure human connect."*

Great products communicate the maker's intent so clearly that users feel **seen**. Design
is not decoration; it is **empathy made visible**.

**The unresolved tension worth holding:** in art, intent is hidden for the audience to
discover. In product design, intent must be *felt* without being spoon-fed. Where is the
balance? This is a sophisticated design question and it does not have a fixed answer —
it is a dial you set per product. Set it consciously.

**Applied:** for anything being designed, ask *what is the intent here, and will the user
feel it?* If the answer is "they'll figure it out," the intent is not in the design yet.

---

## 2. Products should create connection, freedom, and magic

> *"I want to create a profound impact through the products I build — products that
> establish a connection with people, opening new possibilities and bringing them a sense
> of freedom, happiness, and satisfaction."*

> *"Great products don't come from just building features — they emerge when we anticipate
> human needs and shape technology around them."*

The "why" is consistent: technology that feels **magical, human, and expands what people
believe is possible** — for the **next billion users**, not just the already-served.

**Applied:** "we anticipated a need they hadn't articulated" is a higher bar than "we
shipped what they asked for," and it is the bar. Also: when a design decision serves the
already-served at the expense of the next billion, that is a real cost — name it.

---

## 3. Translate data into meaning, not judgment

From the Ribbit Wrapped PRD — the most complete single expression of this philosophy:

> *"None translate money into meaning... You are building a relationship layer between Gen
> Z and their money. The differentiator is Trust + Action."*

The principles embedded there, each of which generalizes far beyond fintech:

- **Insight without shame.** *"Don't shame me"* is a literal, legitimate user goal. Data
  products that judge get abandoned regardless of accuracy.
- **Emotional resonance as a feature**, not as polish applied at the end.
- **Trust earned before action is taken.** *"Wrapped is read-only by design → earns trust
  before action."* The read-only version is not the lesser version; it is the on-ramp.
- **Defensibility through deep context, not gimmicks.** What makes it hard to copy is how
  well you know the user, not the animation.

**Applied:** any product that reflects a user's own behavior back at them — spending,
health, productivity, screen time, performance — must decide deliberately whether it is a
mirror or a judge. Mirrors get used. Judges get deleted.

---

## 4. Agency and willfulness are the scarce resources

> *"Agency, willfulness, and determination will likely be extremely valuable. Correctly
> deciding what to do and figuring out how to navigate an ever-changing world will have
> huge value."*

The future belongs to people who can **decide well and act**. Therefore AI should
**amplify human willfulness, not replace it.**

**Applied — this is the design stance toward AI:** augment, don't automate away the human.
When a design removes a decision from the user, ask whether it removed *friction* or
removed *agency*. Those feel identical in a usability test and are opposite in effect over
months.

---

## 5. Notice invisible problems

> *"Reflecting and identifying problems that seem invisible but are a hazard when done
> wrong are the problems we need to look for... Recognizing problems is something I
> learnt."*

**The archetype: the AC remote.** Setting a temperature (22°C) versus "cooler / more
cooler." One requires you to know what number your body wants; the other speaks the
language of the actual need. Nobody complains about the first one — they have silently
adapted to it. That silence is the signal.

**Applied:** the highest-value problems are the ones users have stopped reporting because
they built a workaround and forgot it was a workaround. Look for:

- Manual steps people perform without noticing
- Spreadsheets and side-channels that exist next to the "real" tool
- Things people apologize for ("sorry, our process is weird")
- Adaptations so old they read as normal

**Recognizing a problem is a skill, and it is trainable.** That is what the perception
habits in `product-sense.md` are for.

---

## 6. Ethics is a design input, not a compliance step

> *"What is good and what is bad? What moves people, drives opinions and decisions... make
> it more difficult to make bad decisions."*

UXR and product cannot dodge the philosophy questions. The design goal is not merely to
enable good decisions — it is to **make bad decisions harder to make**. That is a
structural, architectural commitment, decided early, not a policy page added late.

---

## How to use these

When a decision is close, do not resolve it on effort or feasibility. Resolve it here:

1. Does the user feel the intent?
2. Does this create connection, freedom, or magic — or just function?
3. Does it translate data into meaning, or into judgment?
4. Does it amplify the human's agency, or absorb it?
5. Is this a problem people stopped complaining about?
6. Does it make bad decisions harder?
